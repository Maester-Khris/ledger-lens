**Result:** the kill costs one bucket and nothing hangs. All 67 failures fall in the 15 s of the kill: 60 streams in
flight on the killed replica were cut (they reach k6 as a dropped connection, no headers, so they cannot be tied to a
replica; without the kill there are none), the last **0.12 s** after the kill; 6 nginx 502s (requests already sent to
the dying replica, which nginx does not resend as POSTs); 1 failed `POST /guests`. 0 timeouts, 0 hung requests. On 3
replicas the load moved to the survivors (about 150 turns per bucket each, from about 135) and the extra load came
back as busy replies (19.9% to 42.9%) rather than slower turns: p95 9.26 s during against 9.54 s before. The
restarted replica served its first turn **11.9 s** after `docker start` (container start, app start, nginx retrying
it after `fail_timeout`); the next bucket is a 4-way split again, and there was **no failure after the restart**.
Throughput: 34.61 turns/s before, 29.35 during, 35.69 after. Over the run: 12,436 turns served, 5,005 busy replies
(28.6% of chat requests), 67 failed.

p95 here (about 9.5 s) is above the one-replica runs (about 6.7 s at the knee): every replica ran at about 98% CPU
(`docker stats` median), and the 4 replicas share this machine's 8 cores with k6, the mock (19%), Postgres, Redis and
nginx, so 320 conversations is past the knee of 4 replicas here. Limits: local, one machine; a Compose CPU limit
approximates a vCPU; DB and Redis are local, not a private network; the mock reproduces golden-set timing, not
provider latency or limits; nginx stands in for Railway's edge, whose health checks and retries differ; one run. k6
before this change started a new guest after a busy reply (see the A1 tables); this run used that harness too.
